AI in SMEs: why the problem is not technology, but skills

AI in SMEs is not just about tools. The real obstacles are skills, approach, data and the ability to turn artificial intelligence into practical results.

Workbloom
11 June 2026

Artificial intelligence is now part of the conversation in many businesses: in boardrooms, marketing teams, HR functions, IT departments and meetings between business owners. But the question is no longer simply: “Should we use AI?” A more useful question is: “How can we put AI to practical use without wasting time, budget and attention?” For many SMEs, the main problem is not finding an artificial intelligence tool. The tools exist, are increasingly accessible and are often affordable. The real problem lies elsewhere: a lack of skills, a structured approach and the ability to turn AI into useful business projects. Buying software, launching a chatbot or experimenting with a generative tool does not mean making effective use of AI. To create value, a business needs clear objectives, usable data, well-organised processes and people who can lead change.

AI in SMEs: strong interest, little structured adoption

In recent years, interest in artificial intelligence has grown rapidly. Many companies have started experimenting with generative tools to write copy, summarise documents, analyse data, prepare presentations, or automate repetitive tasks. Yet there is a big difference between experimenting with a tool and integrating AI into business processes. In SMEs, AI adoption often remains stalled for very concrete reasons:

  • it is unclear where to start;
  • internal skills are lacking;
  • data is scattered or unreliable;
  • processes are not mapped;
  • there is no dedicated project owner;
  • there is fear of investing in the wrong tools;
  • risks, limitations, and organisational impacts are unclear.

The result is that many companies talk about AI, but few manage to turn it into a genuine competitive advantage.

The problem is not choosing a tool

Many SMEs begin exploring AI with a seemingly logical question: “Which tool should we buy?” It is an understandable question, but often premature. The market offers many solutions: chatbots, virtual assistants, automation platforms, data analysis tools, customer service software, sales intelligence tools, HR applications, marketing solutions and predictive systems. But a tool alone cannot solve a poorly defined business problem. An SME can buy an advanced platform and achieve no results if:

  • it has not clarified the problem to solve;
  • it does not know which data is needed;
  • it lacks trained people;
  • it has no KPIs to measure impact;
  • it has not defined who governs the project;
  • it has not integrated the tool into actual processes.

AI should start with the business, not the software. The right question is: “Which activity, process or decision do we want to improve with AI?”

The five main obstacles to AI in SMEs

1. A lack of in-house skills

The first obstacle is a lack of people who can assess AI, guide its use and translate it into practical use cases. Not every SME needs to hire data scientists or build an in-house AI team. But it does need someone who can connect technology, processes and business objectives. The skills required are not just technical. Management skills are also needed:

  • understanding where AI can create value;
  • evaluating tools and vendors;
  • engaging business functions;
  • defining priorities;
  • managing risks and data;
  • measuring results;
  • guiding the team through adoption.

Without this guidance, AI risks remaining a collection of isolated experiments.

2. Disorganised data

AI only works well if it can work with reliable data. Many SMEs, however, have information spread across business management software, CRM systems, Excel spreadsheets, emails, shared documents and employees’ informal knowledge. This creates a practical problem: before using AI, the business needs to understand where its data is, how up to date it is, who manages it and whether it is usable. For example, a company wants to use AI to forecast sales, but its CRM is out of date and its sales pipeline is managed subjectively. In that case, the problem is not the algorithm. It is data quality.

3. Unclear processes

AI can automate, speed up or improve a process. But if the process is confused, AI may simply make that confusion happen faster. Before introducing artificial intelligence, an SME should ask:

  • which tasks are repetitive?
  • where is the most time lost?
  • where do errors occur?
  • which decisions are made without data?
  • which steps rely too heavily on individual people?
  • which activities could be simplified?

This analysis is essential. It helps identify where AI can make a practical difference and where it is better to reorganise the process first.

4. No one is responsible for the project

Many AI projects fail because no one is truly responsible for them. IT can manage the technical aspects, but does not always know the sales or operational priorities. Marketing may experiment with generative tools without shared governance. Management may ask people to “do something with AI” without defining objectives, timelines or success criteria. To work, an AI project needs clear ownership. Someone needs to answer questions such as:

  • what is the priority use case?
  • who needs to be involved?
  • what data is required?
  • which vendor or tool do we choose?
  • how do we measure the outcome?
  • what risks do we need to manage?
  • when do we decide whether to scale the project?

SMEs often lack someone in this role internally. That is why it can be useful to bring in an on-demand AI expert, a fractional CTO or a senior manager with experience in digital transformation.

5. Fear of investing in the wrong things

Many business owners are not opposed to AI. They are cautious. They worry about buying tools they do not need, choosing the wrong suppliers, starting overly complex projects, creating data problems or being unable to measure the return on investment. This caution is understandable. The point is to stop it turning into inaction. An SME does not necessarily need to start with a large AI project. It can begin with a narrowly defined use case, clear objectives, a controlled budget and measurable results. The right approach is not to “do everything at once”, but to get off to a good start.

Where AI can create value in an SME

AI should not be introduced simply because it is a trend. It must be tied to real business challenges. Here are several areas where it can generate value.

Sales and marketing

AI can help to:

  • analyse customers and segments;
  • qualify leads;
  • personalise sales messaging;
  • support content creation;
  • summarise calls and meetings;
  • improve follow-ups and proposals;
  • analyse campaign performance.

For an SME in the commercial sector, AI can make the sales team’s work more efficient and improve the quality of its pipeline.

Customer service

In customer service, AI can support:

  • answering frequent enquiries;
  • ticket classification;
  • suggesting responses to agents;
  • complaint analysis;
  • updating knowledge bases;
  • reducing response times.

In many cases, it is not about replacing people, but about reducing repetitive tasks and improving service quality.

Operations and internal processes

In operations, AI can help to:

  • forecast demand and workloads;
  • optimise inventory;
  • analyse inefficiencies;
  • automate document processing;
  • detect anomalies;
  • support planning and quality control.

Even minor operational improvements can have a substantial impact on margins, timelines, and productivity.

Finance and management accounting

In finance, AI can support:

  • cost analysis;
  • forecasting;
  • document classification;
  • reconciliations;
  • management reporting;
  • anomaly detection;
  • scenario simulation.

Here, however, expert oversight is essential. AI can enhance the work of a CFO, controller, or fractional CFO, but it does not replace managerial responsibility for financial decisions.

HR and organisation

In HR, AI can be useful for:

  • drafting job descriptions;
  • analysing skills;
  • supporting onboarding;
  • personalising training paths;
  • analysing internal surveys;
  • improving recruitment processes.

Here too, clear guidelines are essential, as people-related decisions require care, transparency, and accountability.

From experiment to project: the decisive shift

Many businesses have already started using AI informally. Some use it to write emails. Some to summarise documents. Some to prepare presentations. Some to generate ideas or analyse files. These experiments are useful, but they are not enough. The real step forward comes when a company moves from: “Let’s try this tool” to: “Let’s use AI to improve this process, with this objective and these KPIs.” An experiment can be individual. A project needs to involve the organisation. An experiment may have no metrics. A project needs to produce a measurable result. An experiment can depend on one person’s initiative. A project needs to be integrated into the company’s way of working.

How to get started with AI in an SME

1. Carry out an initial assessment

Before choosing tools, it is useful to assess:

  • digital maturity level;
  • data quality;
  • most critical processes;
  • available skills;
  • tools already in use;
  • risks;
  • business priorities.

The aim is to understand where AI can make a difference and where it is too early to introduce it.

2. Choose a small number of use cases

An SME should not launch ten projects at once. It is better to identify two or three high-potential use cases. A sound use case should feature:

  • a clear problem;
  • available data;
  • measurable impact;
  • manageable complexity;
  • an internal sponsor;
  • reasonable timelines.

Examples:

  • reducing customer service response times;
  • improving lead qualification;
  • automating document classification;
  • supporting sales forecasting;
  • speeding up proposal preparation;
  • analysing margins and anomalies.

3. Launch a pilot project

The first AI project should be focused and measurable. It does not need to transform the entire company. It needs to prove value. A sound pilot includes:

  • a defined scope;
  • user involvement;
  • before-and-after metrics;
  • a controlled budget;
  • identified risks;
  • criteria for deciding whether to continue.

4. Train the team

AI does not work if it stays in the hands of a few curious individuals. Practical training is needed on:

  • how to use the tools;
  • which data not to share;
  • how to verify outputs;
  • what limitations to consider;
  • how to integrate AI into daily workflows.

Training reduces fear, errors, and resistance.

5. Set simple rules

SMEs need governance too. There is no need to create bureaucracy, but some rules are essential:

  • which tools may be used;
  • which data must not be uploaded;
  • who approves new use cases;
  • how outcomes are verified;
  • who manages vendors and licences;
  • how sensitive data is protected.

Governing AI does not mean slowing down innovation. It means making it safer and more sustainable.

When you need an on-demand AI expert

An SME does not always need to hire a full-time AI manager. Bringing in senior expertise for a specific phase can often be more effective. An on-demand AI expert can help when the company:

  • wants to understand where to begin;
  • needs to identify concrete use cases;
  • wants to evaluate tools or vendors;
  • needs to set up a pilot project;
  • needs to train the team;
  • wants to define initial governance rules;
  • needs to align AI, processes, and business goals.

In some cases, a fractional CTO may also be needed, particularly when AI is part of a broader project involving digital transformation, systems integration, data management or a review of IT infrastructure. The value of these professionals is not just technical. It is managerial: they help the company turn a complex subject into a practical way forward.

Mistakes to avoid

Here are the most common mistakes an SME should avoid:

  • starting with the tool instead of the problem;
  • delegating everything to IT;
  • underestimating data quality and accessibility;
  • failing to involve the people who will actually use the tool;
  • failing to train the team;
  • not defining KPIs;
  • leaving responsibilities unclear;
  • starting projects that are too large;
  • ignoring privacy, security, and compliance;
  • treating AI as a trend rather than an organisational lever.

The best way to reduce these risks is to start small, but take a structured approach.

FAQs

Is AI useful for SMEs too?

Yes, if applied to concrete problems. It can assist with sales, marketing, customer service, operations, finance, HR, and document management.

What is the first step to introducing AI in a company?

The first step is an assessment: processes, data, skills, existing tools, priorities, and risks. Only then does it make sense to select tools or vendors.

Do you need to hire a full-time AI manager?

Not always. Many SMEs can start with an on-demand AI expert, a fractional CTO, or a senior consultant to define roadmaps and initial pilot projects.

How do you measure the success of an AI project?

It depends on the use case. Potential KPIs include time savings, productivity gains, improved lead quality, error reduction, faster response times, or greater forecast accuracy.

What is the most common mistake?

Starting with the tool instead of the problem. AI generates value only when tied to a clear business objective.

Conclusion

AI in SMEs is no longer a futuristic idea. It is already a way to compete, but only for businesses that can turn experimentation into an organisational capability. The real challenge is not adopting the latest tool available. It is understanding where AI can create value, which data is needed, which processes must change, which skills are missing and who should lead the project. For many SMEs, the problem is therefore not technological. It is managerial. They need someone who can connect business needs, data, tools, people and results.

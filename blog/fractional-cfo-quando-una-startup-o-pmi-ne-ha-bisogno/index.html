Fractional CFO: when a start-up or SME needs one

Find out when a start-up or SME needs a fractional CFO, what they do, which problems they solve and when they make sense compared with a full-time CFO.

Workbloom
11 June 2026

Many start-ups and SMEs reach a point where financial management can no longer be handled solely by an accountant, an in-house administration team and Excel spreadsheets. Growth brings complexity: more customers, higher costs, more people, more investment and more decisions. The business faces more questions that require clear data:

  • How much cash do we really have?
  • How much can we invest over the coming months?
  • Which products or customers generate a margin?
  • When will we break even?
  • Are we ready to apply for financing or launch a funding round?
  • Do we have a reliable management accounting system?
  • Which KPIs do we need to track every month?

At this stage, senior financial expertise may be required, but hiring a full-time CFO right away does not always make sense. A fractional CFO addresses this exact need: bringing experienced financial leadership into the business, with flexible involvement proportionate to the growth phase.

What is a fractional CFO?

A fractional CFO is a senior finance director who works with a business for part of the time that a full-time role would involve. They may work a few days a month, one or more days a week, or on a specific project: fundraising, management accounting, financial reorganisation, reporting, budgeting, business planning or cash management. Their role is not just to “do the sums”. A fractional CFO helps the business turn numbers into decisions. They can support CEOs and founders in areas such as:

  • Financial strategy;
  • Management accounting;
  • Budgeting and forecasting;
  • Cash flow management;
  • Margin analysis;
  • Fundraising;
  • Relations with banks and investors;
  • Structuring KPIs;
  • Executive reporting;
  • Growth planning;
  • Financial risk management.

In short, this is a professional who brings a structured approach, perspective and financial discipline without necessarily joining full-time.

What does a fractional CFO do?

A fractional CFO can support both strategic and operational activities.

1. Sets up management accounting

Many SMEs know their turnover, but do not always have a clear picture of margins, costs or profitability by customer, product, channel or business unit. A fractional CFO helps build a management accounting system that shows where the business makes money, where it loses efficiency and which decisions improve profitability. They can introduce:

  • Management profit and loss statements;
  • Cost-centre analysis;
  • Margins by product or service;
  • Financial dashboards;
  • Profitability KPIs;
  • Monthly reporting;
  • Budget-to-actual variance analysis.

This enables management to make decisions based on data, rather than just impressions.

2. Manages cash flow and liquidity

For a start-up or SME, liquidity is often more critical than short-term profitability. A company can be profitable on paper, but find itself in difficulty if client collections are delayed, suppliers are paid too quickly, or investments and working capital are not properly planned. A fractional CFO helps monitor and forecast cash flow. They can develop:

  • 13-week cash forecasts;
  • 6- to 12-month financial plans;
  • Analysis of receipts and payments;
  • Working capital management;
  • Liquidity scenarios;
  • Alerts about potential cash flow pressures.

The result is a greater ability to anticipate problems rather than be caught out by them.

3. Builds budgets and forecasts

Growing without a budget is like driving a business while looking only in the rear-view mirror. A fractional CFO helps build a realistic budget and an up-to-date forecasting system. This makes it possible to answer crucial questions:

  • What revenue can we expect?
  • Which costs will increase?
  • How much can we invest in marketing, people or technology?
  • Which scenarios do we need to consider?
  • What happens if sales slow down?
  • When will new financing be required?

For start-ups and SMEs, a solid forecast is not a theoretical exercise. It is a decision-making tool.

4. Supports fundraising and investor relations

For a start-up, a fractional CFO can be decisive when preparing for an investment round. They can help build:

  • Business plans;
  • Financial models;
  • Investor pitch decks;
  • SaaS or start-up metrics;
  • Cash runway projections;
  • Cap tables;
  • Growth scenarios;
  • Financial due diligence;
  • Investor reporting.

Founders often know their product, market and vision well, but need a senior professional to make the financial side of the business robust and credible. A fractional CFO helps present consistent figures, verifiable assumptions and a credible financial plan.

5. Improves executive reporting and dashboards

Many businesses have data, but lack genuinely useful information. A fractional CFO helps select a small number of relevant KPIs and present them clearly to management. Examples include:

  • Recurring revenue;
  • Gross margin;
  • EBITDA;
  • Cash burn;
  • Runway;
  • Customer acquisition cost;
  • Customer lifetime value;
  • Average collection period;
  • Margin by customer;
  • Growth rate;
  • Budget-to-actual variance.

The aim is not to fill the business with reports, but to create a dashboard that helps CEOs, founders and managers make better decisions.

6. Works alongside CEOs, founders and management

A fractional CFO does not work only with numbers. They also work alongside the leadership team. They can help the business owner or founder assess:

  • New hires;
  • Investments;
  • Pricing;
  • Partnerships;
  • Entry into new markets;
  • Acquisitions;
  • Cost reductions;
  • Sustainability of growth;
  • Financial structure;
  • The balance between risk and return.

In many SMEs, the CEO makes important financial decisions without a senior colleague to discuss them with. A fractional CFO provides that sounding board, bringing a structured approach and an independent perspective.

When a start-up needs a fractional CFO

A start-up can benefit greatly from a fractional CFO once it moves beyond the initial stage and needs to manage growth, capital, metrics and investors in a more structured way.

1. Ahead of an investment round

If a start-up wants to raise capital, it needs to present solid figures. A fractional CFO can help prepare the financial model, cash plan, metrics, scenarios, cap table and investor materials. Their role is to make the start-up more credible and ready for due diligence.

2. When burn rate is out of control

Many start-ups scale quickly but lack clear visibility into their burn rate, runway, and cost sustainability. A fractional CFO helps answer questions such as:

  • How many months of cash do we have left?
  • What happens if the funding round is delayed?
  • Which expenses can we reduce without stifling growth?
  • Which investments are genuine priorities?
  • What level of growth is sustainable?

3. When the founder is still handling all the finances

In the early stages, it is normal for the founder to handle budgets, payments, metrics and investor updates. But at some point this becomes a bottleneck. A fractional CFO frees up the founder’s time and improves the quality of financial decisions.

4. When more mature metrics are needed

To grow, a start-up needs to monitor the right metrics. Depending on its business model, these may include MRR, ARR, CAC, payback period, gross margin, burn multiple, runway, revenue retention and margins by segment. A fractional CFO helps define which metrics really matter and how to use them to guide growth.

5. When new investors come on board

After an investment, expectations around governance, reporting and transparency increase. A fractional CFO can establish investor reporting, regular updates, KPI monitoring and a process for managing expectations.

When an SME needs a fractional CFO

For an SME, a fractional CFO is useful when financial management becomes too complex to be handled solely by the administration team and external advisers.

1. When the business is growing, but margins are unclear

An SME can increase revenue while simultaneously seeing its profitability decline. This happens when there is a lack of clear visibility into:

  • Indirect costs;
  • Margin by customer;
  • Margin by product;
  • Sales discounts;
  • Production costs;
  • Operational efficiency;
  • Collection times;
  • Outdated prices.

A fractional CFO helps identify where value is really created.

2. When structured management accounting is missing

Many SMEs have accurate accounts, but lack management accounting that is useful to their leadership team. Accounting mainly looks at the past. Management accounting helps inform future decisions. A fractional CFO builds tools to monitor performance, variances, margins and the sustainability of business decisions.

3. When stronger relationships with banks or lenders are required

An SME may need capital to finance investments, business expansion, internationalisation, acquisitions, or working capital. A fractional CFO can support:

  • Financial plans;
  • Business plans suitable for bank funding;
  • Analysis of funding requirements;
  • Preparation of documentation;
  • Negotiations with lenders;
  • Monitoring covenants and financial commitments.

4. When the business owner wants to make data-driven decisions

In many SMEs, decisions still rely on experience, intuition and direct knowledge of the business. These remain valuable, but they are not enough as the business grows. A fractional CFO helps the business owner combine intuition with data, strengthening decisions about investment, prices, staff, suppliers and markets.

5. When the business is preparing for a major transition

A fractional CFO can be useful in situations such as:

  • Succession to the next generation;
  • New shareholders or investors joining the business;
  • Acquiring another company;
  • Selling the business;
  • Opening a new location;
  • Launching a new business unit;
  • Financial reorganisation;
  • A liquidity crisis;
  • A turnaround plan.

At these stages, the quality of financial management can make a difference.

Signs that you may need a fractional CFO

A start-up or SME should consider a fractional CFO when signals like these arise:

  • Turnover is growing, but cash remains under pressure;
  • Margins are unclear;
  • The CEO lacks reliable reports;
  • The budget is prepared once a year and then forgotten;
  • Financial decisions depend on outdated Excel files;
  • The accountant is the only point of reference for the figures;
  • Investors want more structured reporting;
  • The bank requires more robust plans;
  • The administration team handles operations, but not strategy;
  • The company does not know how much it can invest;
  • Costs are growing faster than revenue;
  • The founder spends too much time on finance and administration;
  • There is no credible forecast for the next 6–12 months.

These signs do not necessarily mean you need to hire a full-time CFO immediately. They often indicate you need senior guidance on a flexible, part-time basis.

Fractional CFO or full-time CFO: how to decide

The choice depends on the company’s stage of development, complexity, budget and how ongoing the need is. In general, a fractional CFO is suitable when the financial need is significant, but does not yet justify a full-time role. A full-time CFO is more suitable when the business faces ongoing complexity, has a large finance team, undertakes frequent major corporate transactions or has a more mature management structure.

Fractional CFO, accountant and head of administration: the differences

One of the most common questions is: “If we already have an accountant and someone handling administration, why would we need a fractional CFO?” The answer is that these are different roles.

A fractional CFO does not necessarily replace these professionals. They coordinate their work, bring it together and turn administrative and accounting information into useful insights for management.

What a fractional CFO can do in the first 30, 60 and 90 days

First 30 days: assessment and priorities

During the first month, the fractional CFO can:

  • Review financial statements, cash flow and management accounts;
  • Assess the quality of available data;
  • Identify problems with margins, cash flow and costs;
  • Map existing reporting;
  • Discuss priorities with the CEO, administration team, accountant and investors;
  • Define financial priorities;
  • Build an initial roadmap.

Expected outcomes:

  • Financial assessment;
  • List of priorities;
  • Initial KPIs;
  • Action plan;
  • Visibility of the most urgent risks.

Within 60 days: tools and quick wins

By the second month, the fractional CFO can:

  • Set up cash forecasts;
  • Build a management dashboard;
  • Improve budgeting and reporting;
  • Analyse margins;
  • Review costs and investments;
  • Prepare documentation for banks or investors;
  • Introduce regular financial reviews.

Expected outcomes:

  • Greater control over liquidity;
  • Clearer reports;
  • Faster decisions;
  • Initial action on costs, prices or margins;
  • Greater alignment within management on the figures.

Within 90 days: consolidation and roadmap

By the third month, the fractional CFO can:

  • Establish a consistent monthly reporting process;
  • Consolidate budgets and forecasts;
  • Develop financial scenarios;
  • Pass on working methods to the internal team;
  • Structure investor updates;
  • Support financial negotiations;
  • Define a 6- to 12-month financial plan.

Expected outcomes:

  • A stronger finance function;
  • Consistently monitored KPIs;
  • Greater autonomy for the team;
  • A clearer financial outlook;
  • An informed decision on next steps.

Practical example for a start-up

A B2B SaaS start-up has been operating for 18 months, has some recurring sales and a small team, and aims to raise a seed round. The founder still manages the financial model, cash position and reports for potential investors. The problems are:

  • The financial model is out of date;
  • SaaS metrics are not properly monitored;
  • The burn rate is rising;
  • The pitch deck does not make a convincing case for the business’s sustainability;
  • Runway is unclear;
  • Investors are asking for more robust data.

A fractional CFO can step in to:

  • Review the financial model;
  • Calculate MRR, ARR, churn, CAC, LTV and runway;
  • Develop growth scenarios;
  • Prepare investor reporting;
  • Strengthen the fundraising plan;
  • Support due diligence.

In this case, a fractional CFO is not an administrative cost. They help increase the start-up’s credibility with the financial market.

Practical example for an SME

A manufacturing SME’s turnover grows by 20%, but the business owner notices that cash is always under pressure. The company has an external accountant and an in-house administration function, but lacks sophisticated management accounting. The problems are:

  • Unclear margins by product line;
  • Outdated price lists;
  • Long collection times;
  • Increased indirect costs;
  • No cash forecast;
  • Investment decisions made without scenario analysis.

A fractional CFO can step in to:

  • Analyse margins by product and customer;
  • Set up cash forecasts;
  • Review pricing and discounts;
  • Build a monthly management report;
  • Identify critical costs;
  • Improve banking relationships;
  • Define a financial plan for growth.

In this case, a fractional CFO helps the business owner turn growth from “more turnover” into “more value”.

When you do not need a fractional CFO

A fractional CFO is not always necessary. It may not be the right choice if:

  • The business is very small and only needs administrative support;
  • There are no significant financial decisions to make;
  • The problem is purely tax-related;
  • Management is unwilling to share data and information;
  • There is no willingness to change processes;
  • The business needs someone in a full-time, day-to-day operational role;
  • The company is only looking for someone to handle its bookkeeping.

A fractional CFO is effective when the business needs senior financial guidance, not just administrative support.

How to choose the right fractional CFO

When choosing a fractional CFO, it is important to assess:

  • Experience with businesses of a similar size and stage of development;
  • Sector knowledge;
  • Expertise in start-ups, SMEs, fundraising or corporate finance;
  • Ability to communicate with business owners, founders, banks and investors;
  • Management accounting experience;
  • A practical approach;
  • Ability to build simple, usable systems;
  • Availability that matches the need;
  • Ability to work with the in-house administration team and external advisers.

A good fractional CFO should not just understand finance. They must know how to make it useful for business decisions.

How to prepare for the arrival of a fractional CFO

To gain value quickly, the company should gather some key documents before the engagement begins:

  • Latest available financial statements;
  • An up-to-date picture of financial performance;
  • Turnover and margin data;
  • Current cash position;
  • Customer list and revenue figures;
  • Main costs;
  • Investment plan;
  • Any debts or financing arrangements;
  • Existing reports;
  • Existing budgets or forecasts;
  • Cap table, for start-ups;
  • Investor documents, if available.

Not everything needs to be perfect. In fact, a fractional CFO is often needed precisely because the data is disorganised. But starting with what is available speeds up the work.

Fractional CFO FAQs

What is a fractional CFO?

A fractional CFO is a senior finance director who works with a business on a part-time basis, without being hired full-time. They support financial strategy, management accounting, cash flow, budgeting, forecasting and fundraising.

When do you need a fractional CFO?

A fractional CFO is useful when a start-up or SME needs senior financial expertise, but not necessarily a full-time CFO. Examples include growth, fundraising, cash flow problems, a lack of management accounting or the need for more structured reporting.

How much does a fractional CFO cost?

The cost depends on experience, the time commitment required, duration, complexity and project objectives. In general, the fractional model gives businesses access to senior expertise with an investment proportionate to their needs.

What is the difference between a CFO and an accountant?

An accountant focuses on tax, financial statements and compliance. A CFO focuses on strategic financial management: planning, cash flow, management accounting, KPIs, investment, funding and business decisions.

Can a fractional CFO help a start-up raise capital?

Yes. They can support financial modelling, business planning, metrics, investor presentations, cap tables, reporting and preparation for due diligence.

Does a fractional CFO replace the head of administration?

Not necessarily. They usually work alongside and coordinate the head of administration, turning administrative and accounting data into useful information for management.

Is a fractional CFO or a full-time CFO better?

It depends on the company’s stage of development. A fractional CFO is suitable when senior expertise is needed, but not full-time. A full-time CFO is more appropriate when financial complexity is ongoing and structural.

Conclusion

A fractional CFO is a practical solution for start-ups and SMEs that want to grow with greater control, without immediately hiring a full-time finance director. They can help manage liquidity, budgets, forecasts, margins, fundraising, reporting and strategic decisions. For a start-up, this means presenting a stronger case to investors and keeping track of burn rate and runway. For an SME, it means understanding margins, cash flow and the sustainability of growth. In both cases, the value is not just having better-organised figures. It is making better decisions.

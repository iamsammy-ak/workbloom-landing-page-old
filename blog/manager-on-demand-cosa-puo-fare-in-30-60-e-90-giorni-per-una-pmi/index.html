On-demand manager: what they can do for an SME in 30, 60 and 90 days

Discover what an on-demand manager can do for an SME in the first 30, 60 and 90 days: objectives, activities, expected results and practical examples.

Workbloom
10 June 2026

Many SMEs reach a point where growth slows, processes become more complex or decisions require skills that are not yet available in-house. Senior guidance may be needed to structure sales, improve management accounting, digitise processes, introduce AI, reorganise the team or enter a new market. The difficulty is that hiring a full-time manager straight away does not always make sense. Recruitment can take a long time, costs can be high and the need may relate only to a specific phase. In these cases, an on-demand manager can help an SME access senior management expertise quickly, with flexible involvement focused on clear objectives. But what can an on-demand manager actually do in the first 30, 60 and 90 days?

What is an on-demand manager?

An on-demand manager is a senior professional who joins a business to support a specific function, project, or phase without being hired full-time. They can operate as a:

  • fractional manager;
  • interim manager;
  • hands-on senior consultant;
  • project-based manager;
  • adviser with executive responsibilities.

The defining feature is flexibility: the company brings in the right expertise when needed, for as long as necessary and with a clearly defined brief. Unlike purely strategic consultancy, an on-demand manager works closely with the internal team. They do not simply produce recommendations, but help turn decisions into actions, processes, priorities and measurable results.

Why the on-demand model is useful for SMEs

For an SME, bringing in senior management expertise can be difficult. The business often needs experienced professionals, but:

  • does not have the budget for a full-time C-level executive;
  • does not have time for a lengthy recruitment process;
  • is unsure exactly which role to recruit for;
  • has an urgent problem to solve;
  • needs to manage a temporary period of change;
  • wants to test a new direction before building an internal team.

An on-demand manager meets precisely this need: bringing management experience where it is needed, when it is needed, without adding unnecessary organisational overhead.

What an on-demand manager can do in the first 30 days

The first 30 days are primarily dedicated to diagnosis, prioritisation, and building an action plan. The goal is not to change everything immediately, but to quickly understand where to intervene to generate real impact.

1. Analysing the business situation

The on-demand manager starts with a practical assessment of the business. They may analyse:

  • the business model;
  • internal organisation;
  • decision-making processes;
  • financial and sales figures;
  • the sales pipeline;
  • the marketing structure;
  • digital tools;
  • the team’s skills;
  • operational issues;
  • management’s objectives.

This phase serves to separate symptoms from root causes. For instance, an SME might believe it has a sales problem, only to discover that the real issue stems from positioning, profit margins, pipeline management, or a lack of reliable data.

2. Identifying priorities

An SME cannot afford to spread its efforts across too many areas. In the first 30 days, the on-demand manager therefore helps answer a fundamental question: which two or three priorities could have the greatest impact over the coming months? These priorities may include:

  • increasing the number of qualified leads;
  • improving sales conversion rates;
  • reducing operational inefficiencies;
  • introducing a management accounting system;
  • choosing new software;
  • reviewing roles and responsibilities;
  • preparing a growth plan;
  • improving cash flow and margins;
  • starting a realistic AI project.

The value of an on-demand manager lies not just in "doing", but in deciding what to do first.

3. Defining measurable objectives

An on-demand engagement works when it is clear what it needs to deliver. It is therefore useful to define KPIs and expected outcomes in the first 30 days. Examples include:

  • increasing the number of qualified leads by 20%;
  • shortening sales closing cycles;
  • building a 12-month financial forecast;
  • defining a digital roadmap;
  • reducing repetitive manual tasks;
  • improving margins on specific product lines;
  • introducing a monthly reporting framework;
  • redesigning the HR onboarding process.

Objectives must be realistic, yet clear enough to steer subsequent work.

4. Building an operational roadmap

By the end of the first month, the on-demand manager should deliver a simple, concrete roadmap. Not a theoretical document, but an operational plan detailing:

  • priorities;
  • activities;
  • internal owners;
  • timelines;
  • risks;
  • dependencies;
  • quick wins;
  • KPIs;
  • review milestones.

This roadmap becomes the operational foundation for the subsequent 60 days.

Expected outcomes in the first 30 days

After the first month, an SME should have:

  • greater clarity on underlying issues;
  • defined priorities;
  • measurable objectives;
  • a shared action plan;
  • initial high-impact actions underway;
  • alignment between leadership and the team.

In short: in the first 30 days, the on-demand manager brings order, focus and a structured approach.

What an on-demand manager can achieve within 60 days

Between days 30 and 60, the focus shifts from diagnosis to execution. The goal is to start implementing priority actions, engage the team, and generate early, tangible results.

1. Launching early operational interventions

Depending on their area of expertise, the on-demand manager can start working on practical tasks. A sales and marketing manager might:

  • review market positioning;
  • structure the sales funnel;
  • improve lead qualification;
  • define scripts, processes and metrics;
  • align marketing and sales;
  • introduce more effective sales meetings.

A fractional CFO might:

  • set up cash flow planning and forecasting;
  • review margins and costs;
  • build financial performance dashboards;
  • prepare reporting for management or investors;
  • improve management accounting and financial control.

A fractional CTO or IT manager might:

  • assess systems and suppliers;
  • identify digital inefficiencies;
  • define a technology roadmap;
  • help select tools;
  • establish governance for digital projects.

An on-demand HR manager might:

  • clarify roles and responsibilities;
  • improve onboarding and performance management;
  • support recruitment for critical roles;
  • help manage organisational change;
  • structure core HR processes.

2. Engaging the internal team

A good on-demand manager does not work in isolation. Their role also involves developing the internal team, bringing a structured approach and strengthening decision-making. In the first 60 days, they can:

  • clarify who decides what;
  • assign responsibilities;
  • introduce regular working routines;
  • improve communication between functions;
  • create shared dashboards;
  • train key people;
  • reduce reliance on the business owner or founder.

This is particularly important in SMEs, where many decisions remain concentrated in the hands of a few people.

3. Delivering measurable quick wins

Within 60 days, it is realistic to expect some initial tangible results. These will not always be final financial results, but they can be important signs of improvement. Examples of quick wins include:

  • a more organised sales pipeline;
  • a clear, readable financial forecast;
  • a dashboard tracking core KPIs;
  • clearer internal workflows;
  • a reduction in manual work;
  • more informed vendor selection;
  • better project prioritisation;
  • more targeted marketing campaigns;
  • stronger alignment between management and the team.

Quick wins have a twofold value: they make an impact and build confidence in the process.

4. Correcting what isn't working

The initial roadmap should not be rigid. After 60 days, the on-demand manager can assess what is working and what is not. They can then:

  • review priorities;
  • eliminate unnecessary activities;
  • adjust the plan;
  • reallocate resources;
  • redefine KPIs;
  • clarify newly identified issues.

This pragmatic approach is particularly valuable for SMEs and startups, where conditions change rapidly and decisions must adapt quickly.

Expected outcomes within 60 days

After two months, an SME should see:

  • initial actions implemented;
  • a more engaged team;
  • clearer processes;
  • more understandable data;
  • operational quick wins;
  • better decision-making;
  • greater control over priorities.

In short: within 60 days, the on-demand manager turns the plan into execution.

What an on-demand manager can achieve within 90 days

Between days 60 and 90, the work starts to become established. The aim is to turn the initial actions into a more stable system.

1. Embedding processes and responsibilities

After 90 days, the business should not depend solely on the on-demand manager's presence. The work must start becoming part of the company's internal capabilities. To achieve this, the manager can:

  • document processes;
  • define roles and responsibilities;
  • establish monitoring routines;
  • set up regular meetings;
  • transfer skills;
  • train internal points of contact;
  • make the new activities repeatable.

This is one of the most critical steps: an on-demand manager should not create dependency, but rather increase the company's ability to manage its own priorities.

2. Measuring results

Within 90 days, it is possible to assess the initial impact of the engagement. Results can vary considerably depending on the objective, but they should be measurable. Examples include:

  • higher sales pipeline quality;
  • improved visibility into cash flow and margins;
  • reduced operational inefficiencies;
  • faster decision-making cycles;
  • stronger governance over digital initiatives;
  • a clearer commercial strategy;
  • greater autonomy across the team;
  • recruitment or onboarding of key hires;
  • validation of a new market or channel.

Not every financial result shows up within 90 days, but within 90 days it must be clear whether the business is moving in the right direction.

3. Deciding the next step

At the end of the first 90 days, the company can make an informed decision. Possible options include:

  • continue working with the on-demand manager;
  • reduce their involvement;
  • turn the role into an ongoing fractional arrangement;
  • hire someone in-house;
  • bring in another senior professional;
  • close the project because the objectives have been achieved.

This is one of the main advantages of the on-demand model: it allows the business to test and adjust the level of expertise it needs without rigid commitments.

4. Building a medium-term roadmap

After 90 days, the on-demand manager can help the business shape its plan for the next 6 to 12 months. They can outline:

  • which skills are still missing;
  • which processes need strengthening;
  • which investments should take priority;
  • which risks need managing;
  • which KPIs to monitor;
  • which roles to recruit in-house;
  • which activities can remain on-demand.

In this way, the engagement becomes part of a more solid growth path rather than remaining an isolated exercise.

Expected outcomes within 90 days

After three months, an SME should have:

  • more structured processes;
  • clearer responsibilities;
  • KPIs being monitored;
  • initial evidence of impact;
  • a more autonomous team;
  • more informed decisions;
  • a defined roadmap for the next stage.

In short: within 90 days, the on-demand manager consolidates change and guides the company on what to do next.

Practical example: an on-demand manager for a sales-focused SME

Imagine a B2B SME with stagnant sales, a poorly structured marketing function and heavy reliance on the business owner.

First 30 days

The on-demand manager analyses:

  • the sales process;
  • the pipeline;
  • customer acquisition channels;
  • offers;
  • market positioning;
  • margins by customer;
  • the sales team’s skills.

Outputs:

  • problem diagnosis;
  • commercial priorities;
  • restructured pipeline;
  • core KPIs;
  • action plan.

Within 60 days

The manager starts work on:

  • a review of the sales process;
  • a new lead qualification system;
  • weekly sales meetings;
  • improvements to scripts and follow-up;
  • alignment with marketing;
  • a sales dashboard.

Outputs:

  • sales that are easier to track;
  • a more focused team;
  • less effort wasted on unqualified leads;
  • better visibility of opportunities.

Within 90 days

The manager consolidates:

  • the sales approach;
  • the team’s responsibilities;
  • reporting;
  • priority given to the most profitable customers;
  • a sales development roadmap.

Outputs:

  • a repeatable process;
  • a stronger pipeline;
  • better-informed management;
  • a decision on whether to hire an in-house sales manager or engage a fractional CMO.

Practical example: an on-demand manager for digital transformation

An SME wants to digitise its processes but does not know where to start. It uses various tools and many Excel files, relies on manual tasks and has suppliers who are not coordinated.

First 30 days

The on-demand manager:

  • maps processes and tools;
  • identifies bottlenecks;
  • analyses costs and risks;
  • assesses internal skills;
  • defines digital priorities.

Outputs:

  • a digital assessment;
  • a map of inefficiencies;
  • priorities based on impact and complexity;
  • an initial roadmap.

Within 60 days

The manager:

  • selects tools or suppliers;
  • establishes project governance;
  • eliminates repetitive manual tasks;
  • defines internal roles;
  • launches an initial pilot project.

Outputs:

  • a digital project underway;
  • clear responsibilities;
  • less operational confusion;
  • more informed technology decisions.

Within 90 days

The manager:

  • measures the initial results;
  • documents processes;
  • shares working methods with the team;
  • defines future investments;
  • assesses the new skills required.

Outputs:

  • more orderly digitisation;
  • less waste;
  • a realistic 6–12-month plan;
  • greater control over suppliers.

When to bring in an on-demand manager

An on-demand manager can be useful when a company faces one of the following situations:

  • disorganised growth;
  • stagnant or unpredictable sales;
  • a lack of financial control;
  • difficulty making technology decisions;
  • a need to integrate AI or digital tools;
  • the sudden departure of a key manager;
  • entry into a new market;
  • a need to prepare for fundraising;
  • a young or poorly structured internal team;
  • a lack of senior expertise in a critical function.

The clearest indicator is this: the business recognises a major problem, but lacks the internal expertise to solve it systematically.

When an on-demand manager is not enough

An on-demand manager is not a silver bullet. The arrangement may fall short when:

  • leadership lacks genuine commitment;
  • there are no internal team members available to collaborate;
  • the organisation resists adapting its processes or habits;
  • the challenge demands an immediate, full-time hire;
  • objectives remain vague and undefined;
  • the company is merely seeking cheap operational execution.

For the model to work, it requires clarity, collaboration and a willingness to make decisions.

How to prepare for an on-demand manager's arrival

To gain value quickly, an SME should prepare some information before the engagement begins.

1. Define the problem

You do not need to have the solution figured out, but you must clearly define the underlying need.

2. Gather key data

Depending on the area, this may include: financial statements; forecasts; sales data; the sales pipeline; an organisation chart; current processes; tools in use; available KPIs; ongoing projects; known issues.

3. Involve key stakeholders

The on-demand manager must be able to speak to the people who really understand the problem. It is therefore useful to involve: the business owner or CEO; department heads; the operational team; finance; sales; HR; IT; any key suppliers.

What this means for your business

For an SME, bringing in an on-demand manager means not being stuck between two extremes: doing everything in-house despite lacking the necessary skills, or hiring someone full-time straight away. There is a third option: involving a senior manager for a specific phase, with clear objectives and a level of commitment proportionate to the need. In 30 days, they can bring clarity. In 60 days, they can begin implementation. In 90 days, they can embed a structured approach, processes and decisions. The value is not simply having “an extra person”, but accessing the right expertise when it can really make a difference.

On-demand manager FAQs

What is an on-demand manager?

An on-demand manager is a senior professional who supports a business with a specific function, project or phase without being hired full-time.

What can an on-demand manager do in 30 days?

In the first 30 days, they can analyse the situation, identify priorities, define KPIs and build an operational roadmap.

What can an on-demand manager achieve in 60 days?

Within 60 days, they can start implementing practical actions, involve the internal team, introduce processes and deliver measurable quick wins.

What can an on-demand manager achieve in 90 days?

Within 90 days, they can embed processes, measure results, transfer skills to the team and define the next roadmap.

What is the difference between an on-demand manager and a fractional manager?

On-demand manager is a broad category. A fractional manager is a type of on-demand manager who works for a proportion of the time, often on an ongoing basis.

When is it worth bringing in an on-demand manager?

It makes sense when senior expertise is needed for growth, finance, sales, digitisation, AI, HR, operations or organisational change, but an immediate full-time hire is not necessary.

Does an on-demand manager replace a management consulting firm?

Not always. It depends on the need. Compared with traditional consultancy, however, an on-demand manager often works more closely with the internal team and is more involved in implementation.

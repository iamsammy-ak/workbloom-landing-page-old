Fractional manager: who they are, what they do and when you need one

Discover what a fractional manager is, what they do and when it makes sense to bring one into your business. A practical guide for SMEs, start-ups and growing companies.

Workbloom
10 June 2026

Not every business needs to hire a full-time manager straight away. Sometimes senior expertise is needed only for a specific phase: structuring sales growth, setting up management accounting, leading a digital project, strengthening the internal organisation or preparing the company for a strategic transition. In these cases, a fractional manager can be an effective solution: a senior professional who joins the company with a clear management role, but with a flexible commitment proportionate to the actual need. For SMEs, start-ups and growing businesses, fractional management provides access to high-level expertise without immediately taking on the costs, recruitment times and commitments of a full-time hire.

What is a fractional manager?

A fractional manager is an experienced manager who works with a company for a proportion of the time required by a full-time role. They may be involved for a few days a month, a few days a week or a defined period, with operational and strategic responsibility for a specific business function. Unlike a traditional consultant, a fractional manager does not simply make recommendations. They engage in decision-making, work with the internal team, establish processes, lead projects and contribute to achieving practical objectives. In practice, the role sits between:

  • a full-time manager;
  • an interim manager;
  • a senior consultant;
  • a strategic adviser.

The main difference is flexibility: the company accesses senior management expertise only for the time and level of involvement it needs.

What does a fractional manager do?

A fractional manager’s role depends on the business area they join and the project’s objectives. For example, they may be responsible for:

  • defining a sales strategy;
  • reorganising the marketing team;
  • setting up management accounting;
  • leading a digital roadmap;
  • supporting fundraising;
  • structuring HR processes;
  • improving operations and the supply chain;
  • preparing entry into a new market;
  • supporting founders, CEOs or senior management;
  • managing a period of organisational change.

The key point is that a fractional manager does not work “outside” the company, but alongside it. They bring a structured approach, experience, decision-making skills and an external perspective, with operational involvement much closer to that of an in-house manager.

Examples of fractional managers

Fractional CFO

A fractional CFO supports the company with finance, management accounting, budgeting, reporting, cash flow, fundraising and relationships with investors or banks. They are particularly useful for start-ups and SMEs that have moved beyond the initial stage but do not yet need, or cannot yet hire, a full-time CFO. Practical example: a start-up needs to prepare for an investment round but lacks a sound financial structure. A fractional CFO can establish metrics, forecasts, a business plan and materials for investors.

Fractional CMO

A fractional CMO leads marketing, positioning, lead generation, branding, go-to-market planning and alignment between marketing and sales. They are useful when a company invests in marketing but lacks clear direction or senior in-house expertise. Practical example: an SME has a website, active campaigns and a sales team, but generates few qualified leads. A fractional CMO can review its strategy, messaging, channels, funnel and metrics.

Fractional CTO

A fractional CTO helps a company make technology decisions, digitise processes, select suppliers, assess software architectures or introduce digital and AI tools. They are useful when a company needs technology leadership but cannot yet hire an in-house CTO. Practical example: a company wants to integrate AI tools into its processes but does not know where to start. A fractional CTO or AI expert can assess priorities, risks, available data and an implementation roadmap.

Fractional HR Manager

A fractional HR manager supports a company with organisation, recruitment, performance management, people development, company culture and HR processes. They are useful during growth, reorganisation or the transition from informal management to a more structured management approach. Practical example: an SME is growing rapidly but lacks clear processes for roles, objectives and responsibilities. A fractional HR manager can establish job descriptions, appraisal systems and onboarding processes.

Fractional Head of Growth

A fractional Head of Growth works on business growth, bringing together marketing, sales, product, pricing, retention and data analysis. The role is particularly suited to start-ups, scale-ups and digital businesses. Practical example: a start-up has a good product but struggles to scale customer acquisition. A fractional Head of Growth can identify the most effective channels, test assumptions and build a measurable growth system.

When do you need a fractional manager?

A fractional manager is useful when a company has a genuine management need, but not necessarily a permanent one. The model is suitable when:

1. The business is growing, but lacks a complete managerial structure

Many SMEs and start-ups grow thanks to the vision of their business owner or founders, but eventually need more specialist expertise. Sales, finance, marketing, HR, operations and technology can no longer be managed solely on an informal basis. At this stage, a fractional manager helps introduce a structured approach without adding too much organisational overhead.

2. Senior expertise is needed, but not full-time

It does not always make sense to hire a full-time CFO, CMO, CTO or HR director. The need may be real but concentrated at certain times of the month or on specific projects. A fractional manager provides access to a senior professional even when the budget, business stage or workload does not justify a full-time hire.

3. There is a strategic project to accelerate

A company may need to enter a new market, review its sales strategy, digitise processes, integrate AI, prepare for an investment round or improve management accounting and financial control. These projects require experience and the ability to implement plans. A fractional manager can join with a clear brief, work towards the objectives and transfer skills to the internal team.

4. A key manager leaves the company

When a key manager unexpectedly leaves the organisation, decisions, projects and operational continuity are at risk of being disrupted. In these cases, a fractional manager can temporarily fill the role, ensuring stability and supporting the company until a new hire or reorganisation.

5. The company needs to make complex decisions

Decisions such as entering a new market, changing business management software, reorganising the sales team, introducing AI tools or opening a new line of business require experience. A fractional manager brings an external perspective while taking real responsibility for results.

Fractional manager and interim manager: what is the difference?

Fractional management and interim management are related models, but they are not identical. An interim manager joins a company for a limited period, often with intensive involvement, to manage a transition, crisis, temporary replacement or specific project. A fractional manager, by contrast, works for a proportion of the time, often on an ongoing but not full-time basis. They may support the company for a few months or longer, with a commitment tailored to its needs. Both models can be useful. The choice depends on urgency, how long the need will last, the level of responsibility required and the available budget.

Fractional manager or consultant: what is the difference?

A consultant analyses a problem, proposes solutions, and supports the organisation with specialist insights. A fractional manager, however, takes on a more operational, managerial role. While the boundaries can blur, in general:

  • a consultant advises;
  • a fractional manager leads;
  • a consultant produces analysis and recommendations;
  • a fractional manager helps with implementation;
  • a consultant often works on a project basis;
  • a fractional manager can support the company on an ongoing basis.

This makes a fractional manager particularly useful when a company needs not just ideas, but also direction, a structured approach and implementation.

Which businesses can benefit from a fractional manager?

Fractional management is especially well suited to:

SMEs

SMEs often need senior expertise but cannot always afford or justify hiring a full-time manager. A fractional manager can help with sales growth, finance, organisation, digitisation, operations and succession between generations.

Start-ups

Start-ups need management expertise from an early stage, but must control their burn rate and retain flexibility. A fractional CFO, CMO, CTO or Head of Growth can support growth, fundraising, metrics, go-to-market planning and scalability.

Scale-ups

Scale-ups need to move from rapid but often disorganised growth to a more solid structure. A fractional manager can bring processes, governance, KPIs and the ability to implement plans.

Companies backed by funds or investors

Funds, business angels and investors can use fractional managers to support portfolio companies in critical areas: finance, sales, operations, marketing, HR or digital transformation.

Mature companies undergoing transformation

Established companies may also need senior expertise for specific projects: entering a new market, digital transformation, ESG, compliance, reorganisation or business development.

The benefits of fractional management

Rapid access to senior expertise

The first benefit is the ability to bring in experienced managers without lengthy recruitment processes. When the need is urgent, time is a competitive factor.

Flexibility

The company can adjust the duration, intensity and scope of the engagement. This makes the model more suitable for dynamic environments than hiring someone immediately.

Costs aligned with actual needs

A full-time manager may be too costly or more than the business needs at certain stages. A fractional manager allows the company to pay for the level of involvement it actually requires.

Operational impact

A good fractional manager does not stop at strategy. They help turn decisions into actions, processes, responsibilities and measurable results.

Knowledge transfer

A fractional manager can develop the internal team, bringing working methods and skills that remain in the company after the project ends.

When a fractional manager is not the right choice

Fractional management is not always the best fit. It may not be suitable when:

  • the company needs a daily, ongoing presence;
  • the role requires full-time operational management;
  • objectives are unclear;
  • the internal team is unavailable to collaborate;
  • the company only wants people to carry out tasks, not management guidance;
  • the problem is structural and requires a permanent hire.

Fractional management works best when there is a clear need, a defined brief and strong internal support.

How to choose the right fractional manager

Selecting a fractional manager requires assessing not only their credentials, but also their fit with your business environment. Key criteria to consider include:

  • experience in companies of a similar size or stage;
  • specialist expertise in the required area;
  • the ability to work with business owners, founders or internal teams;
  • a focus on results;
  • clarity about working methods;
  • availability that matches the need;
  • the ability to transfer skills, not just carry out tasks;
  • cultural compatibility with the company.

The question should not simply be: “Is this professional competent?” The right question is: “Are they the most suitable person for our problem, at this stage, with this team and these objectives?”

What this means for your business

Choosing a fractional manager means changing how you access expertise. You do not necessarily need to hire a full-time manager straight away. Nor do you need to opt for a traditional consulting project if what you need is a senior professional working alongside the team. You can bring in the right expertise at the right time, with a level of involvement proportionate to your business stage. For an SME, this can mean growing in a more structured way. For a start-up, it can mean structuring finance, marketing or technology without increasing its burn rate. For a company undergoing transformation, it can mean accelerating a critical project with experienced guidance. The point is not to have more consultants. It is to have the right management expertise when it is really needed.

Fractional manager FAQs

What does fractional manager mean?

A fractional manager is a senior professional who works with a company for part of the time rather than as a full-time manager. They may support a function, a project or a specific phase.

How much does a fractional manager cost?

The cost depends on seniority, area of expertise, duration, level of involvement and project complexity. It is generally more flexible than hiring a full-time manager.

What is the difference between a fractional manager and an interim manager?

A fractional manager works part-time or for a proportion of the time, often on an ongoing basis. An interim manager joins for a defined period, often with more intensive involvement, to manage transitions, temporary replacements or critical projects.

Is a fractional manager a consultant?

Not exactly. A consultant often analyses and advises. A fractional manager takes a role closer to management, works alongside the team and contributes to implementation.

When is it worth bringing in a fractional manager?

It makes sense when senior expertise is needed, but not necessarily full-time: for growth, fundraising, digitisation, reorganisation, business development, management accounting or the launch of new projects.

Conclusion

A fractional manager offers a practical response to an increasingly common challenge: companies need senior management expertise, but cannot or do not always need to make it a permanent part of their workforce. For SMEs, start-ups and growing businesses, this model combines experience, flexibility and speed.
